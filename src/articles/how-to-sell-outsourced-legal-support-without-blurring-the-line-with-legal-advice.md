Legal buyers can smell loose language immediately. Say "cheap lawyers," "we handle everything" or "privilege is covered," and the call may be over before the actual service is understood.

The offer becomes credible when the work is precise, the boundary is hard and qualified counsel keeps the judgment and approval the matter requires.

That discipline protects the buyer, the provider and the credibility of the first conversation.

## Define the work without pretending

"Legal support" can describe contract administration, document review support, matter intake, research assistance, data organization, discovery support and many other processes. It does not automatically mean legal advice.

Before building the campaign, document the exact work, jurisdictions, personnel, supervision, systems, data locations and approvals the provider can support. Write down the excluded work as clearly as the included work.

The caller needs one safe sentence that describes the service. If the sentence sounds broader than the delivery model, tighten it before anyone touches the phone.

## Sell capacity around a workstream

Legal departments rarely buy "more people" in the abstract. They respond to a contract backlog, matter spike, investigation, acquisition, regulatory project or repeat process pulling qualified counsel away from work only they can own.

Choose one workstream for the campaign. A contract-support offer should qualify agreement types, volume, templates, playbooks, systems and review ownership. A matter-support offer should define the administrative steps, deadlines, data and escalation. A law-firm support campaign may need a completely different buyer and supervision model.

Specific work earns a specific conversation. Generic capacity earns risk questions and price shopping.

## Use public signals carefully

Useful signals include a verified acquisition, legal-operations hire, announced regulatory project, public litigation, firm expansion or legal hiring constraint. These events can change workload, but they do not reveal the buyer's confidential needs.

## Copy this legal-support opener

Keep the question on workload and ownership.

> Hi [First Name], [Your Name] with [Company]. Quick question. Is repeat contract, document or matter-support work pulling internal counsel into steps that a controlled support team could handle?

If the buyer says no, respect the answer. If a workstream is named, ask what must stay with counsel and which controls would decide whether external support is even eligible.

## Ask four boundary questions

The first call should expose the work and the stop signs.

1. Which workstream is absorbing the most internal time?
2. Is the need recurring, tied to one matter or caused by a temporary spike?
3. What must remain with internal or qualified counsel, and who keeps final approval?
4. Which jurisdiction, confidentiality, privilege, system-access or quality requirements control the work?

Do not ask the caller to solve detailed legal questions. Capture the requirement and move it to the provider's qualified owner.

## Build the value bridge around control

The value is not "the same legal work for less." The value may be controlled capacity for a defined process, better use of internal counsel, faster movement through a backlog or a consistent operating workflow.

Explain supervision, work instructions, access, review, escalation and final approval in simple terms. Rules differ by jurisdiction and service. For example, the Solicitors Regulation Authority's code emphasizes professional duties including client interests, confidentiality and proper service. The provider must confirm the standards that apply to its own work and buyers.

Cost matters after the buyer believes the control model.

## Handle the legal objections directly

**"We do not outsource legal work."**

"Understood. This would only be relevant if one defined support process could move without shifting legal judgment or internal ownership."

**"Our outside counsel handles overflow."**

"That may be the right model for legal advice. Are they also the best operating fit for the repeatable support work surrounding the matter?"

**"Confidentiality is too sensitive."**

"That has to be qualified before scope. Which data, access, location and personnel requirements would determine whether the model is eligible?"

**"Quality would be hard to control."**

"Agreed. The next discussion should define instructions, reviewer qualifications, sampling, escalation and who retains final approval."

## Qualify the controlled first step

A qualified opportunity has one potentially eligible workstream, enough volume or urgency, an internal legal owner, a serviceable jurisdiction and a willingness to discuss controls. Capture systems, data location, confidentiality, review standards and timing.

The next step may be an eligibility review, workflow scoping call, security discussion or controlled pilot design. It should not be a generic capabilities presentation.

Stop when the work falls outside the provider's approved service, required supervision is absent or a mandatory confidentiality or jurisdiction condition cannot be met.

## Build a handoff legal leaders can trust

The CRM note should record the workstream, volume range, current team, outside counsel, jurisdictions, systems, data location, deadlines, privilege and confidentiality concerns, reviewer, decision group and promised follow-up. Mark every unanswered legal or security point.

Separate public facts, buyer statements and caller assumptions. Never record a guess as if legal leadership confirmed it.

The handoff succeeds when the provider can enter the meeting ready to test scope and controls without correcting the caller's language first.
