“I don’t own this and can’t refer you” closes two routes at once. The person is telling you they do not handle the topic and cannot help you reach someone who does. A good response should respect both points.

This guide starts after the referral path is closed. If the person can still direct you to a role or colleague, use the [general wrong-person guide](/articles/not-the-right-person-cold-call-objection/). Here, the useful work happens in the contact record and subsequent research, after the call ends.

## What should you say when the contact cannot refer you?

Keep the response brief and release the person from the conversation. You do not need another question to make the call useful.

> Understood. Thanks for letting me know. I’ll correct my notes and let you go.

This is an illustrative response, not a tested formula for obtaining a hidden referral. Its purpose is to close respectfully and avoid repeating a targeting mistake. Follow through by correcting the record rather than leaving the contact in the same campaign.

If the person also asks for no more calls, acknowledge and record that preference. Do not interpret the request as permission to try a different channel. Use your campaign’s contact-preference process before any further action.

## Accept what is known and leave the rest open

The contact has corrected your assumption about their responsibility. That does not automatically prove the company needs your product, has no need or has no relevant buyer. Keep the contact outcome separate from the account assessment.

| Statement | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “I don’t own this.” | This contact denies responsibility | No one in the company owns it |
| “I can’t refer you.” | This referral route is unavailable | Another contact endorses your offer |
| “I don’t know who handles it.” | The person lacks routing information | The company has no process |
| “Please stop calling me.” | A clear contact preference | Permission to continue elsewhere by assumption |

Do not ask the person to explain why they cannot help. They may lack information, work in another business unit or simply prefer not to provide a route. None of those possibilities requires the caller to challenge the answer.

## Avoid turning a closed referral into a negotiation

Questions such as “surely you know someone” add pressure after the contact has already answered. Rephrasing the same request as a department, email address or manager’s name can have the same effect. The route remains closed until the person voluntarily offers something new.

Avoid arguing from a job title or a data provider’s record. The person has more direct knowledge of their own responsibilities than your prospect list does. Treat the discrepancy as a research issue to resolve later.

[ZoomInfo’s cold-call objection guide](https://pipeline.zoominfo.com/sales/cold-call-objection-handling) distinguishes useful referral requests from situations where a genuine constraint should be accepted. Its examples also ask permission before mentioning a contact’s name. That is practitioner guidance; it does not establish that every wrong-person response can produce an introduction.

## Correct the contact record before another sequence starts

Update the campaign role or routing status so another caller can understand the correction. Preserve the original note and add the confirmed outcome. Avoid deleting useful history just to make the record look clean.

Useful fields include the topic discussed, the contact’s stated lack of ownership, the unavailable referral path and any request about further contact. Separate facts from the caller’s proposed next research step.

An illustrative note could read: “Contact said they do not own equipment maintenance and cannot direct vendors. No referral or name provided. Remove from this maintenance campaign; account ownership remains unverified.” Add any further contact preference exactly as expressed.

## Reassess whether the account still fits

Before finding another person, check whether the company is a plausible customer for the offer. A wrong contact can reveal a deeper list problem. You may have selected the wrong subsidiary, location or business model.

For example, a property brand may not control maintenance for every building carrying its name. A software group may centralize purchases in one unit while another unit manages daily use. Public company structure can help you form a better hypothesis, but it still needs confirmation.

Use the [B2B buying committee guide](/articles/map-b2b-buying-committee/) when several functions may share responsibility. The aim is to identify the work and its likely owner, not simply to find a more senior title with a reachable phone number.

## Research the responsible function independently

Start with the company’s current website, team pages, public job descriptions and vendor information. Look for the responsibility connected to your offer. A facilities service belongs with facilities or property operations more often than with a generic executive list.

Record where you found the clue and when you checked it. Job descriptions and announcements can be outdated, and similar titles may own different work in different companies. Use the clue to select a careful opening question.

If the company publishes a supplier portal or vendor contact route, follow the stated process. The [transparent gatekeeper guide](/articles/how-to-get-past-b2b-gatekeeper-without-sounding-deceptive/) covers access conversations. A closed referral is not a reason to imply an internal relationship to someone controlling access.

## Start the next call without claiming an introduction

When independent research identifies another plausible contact, explain your own reason for reaching out. Do not borrow the first person’s name to make the call sound invited.

> Hi [Name], [Your Name] from [Company]. I’m reaching out about [specific responsibility] because [verified business reason]. Does your team handle that?

If the new contact asks how you found them, answer accurately. You can describe a public source you actually used. Do not say a colleague recommended the conversation when the colleague declined to refer you.

A direction, permission to mention a name and an active introduction are different things. In this scenario none was given. The next call must earn its own relevance rather than inherit authority from a conversation that closed.

## Keep referral metrics separate from research corrections

| Outcome | Count it as | Do not count it as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wrong owner, no route | Contact correction | Referral |
| Independently identified role | Research hypothesis | Confirmed decision-maker |
| New contact confirms responsibility | Verified ownership | Qualified opportunity by itself |
| Buyer confirms need and meeting purpose | Potential qualified appointment under your standard | Guaranteed sales pipeline |

The [sales handoff guide](/articles/how-to-hand-off-qualified-appointment-from-sdr-to-sales/) explains the context a seller needs after qualification. A replacement contact name is only one input. Need, fit, constraints and an agreed purpose still have to be established.

Review repeated no-referral outcomes by list source, title and account segment. A pattern may suggest that the campaign targets the wrong function. Changing the list can be more useful than teaching callers another way to ask the same unwilling contact.

## Use a clear stop rule and an accountable next action

End this contact’s referral attempt when they say they cannot help. Honor any broader request to stop contact through the campaign’s established process. If account-level relevance remains uncertain, assign a research task with a specific question to resolve before further outreach.

Good research can conclude that the account should leave the campaign. It can also find a legitimate new route that still needs qualification. Neither result requires pretending the first contact was helpful when they were unable to provide a referral.
